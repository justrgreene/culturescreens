SAMLA paper draft — Jordan Peele’s cinema

The Powerful Gaze: Speculative Vision in Jordan Peele’s Cinema

Paper draftDownload Markdown ↓

(Don’t read) Over the past few years, we’ve had an explosion of popular speculative fiction media products. From the success of streaming adaptations like The Last of Us (HBO, 2023) and Fallout (Amazon Prime, 2024) to hugely successful original cinematic works such as Everything, Everywhere All at Once (A24, 2022) and cinematic adaptations of more “traditional” speculative media like Dune (Warner Bros., 2021 and 2024), we are firmly in a golden age of speculative visual media. It may have something to do with the ever-present dangers humanity faces around the globe—climate change, political unrest, wars, and technological fears to name a few. It may be that we, as a culture, have finally realized the artistry and appeal to all audiences within this genre, or more cynically, it may be because production companies and media conglomerates have realized that they can capitalize significantly on these media products. Regardless of the reasons, now is an excellent time to not only be a fan of speculative media but also turn a critical eye toward it to uncover the ways these artists and texts attempt to make familiar the unfamiliar.

Cinematic fictions, particularly those that fall within the broad speculative genre, attempt to use vision to create representations and identities that feel both corporeal and abstract, and by manipulating point-of-view (POV), directors and cinematographers create a narrative environment that refracts lived experiences. If we can accept that, as Murray Smith states in “Imagining from the Inside,” “[w]hat we are to imagine seeing is given directly to us by the camera’s field of vision” (420), that almost any shot within the frame can be “read” as stemming from some point-of-view, whether that be the director’s, the cinematographer’s, the narrator’s, or the characters’. This falls into what Gregory Currie in Image and Mind terms “The Imagined Observer Hypothesis,” which posits that by functioning as our primary method of “seeing” in cinema, the camera’s point-of-view narrates the film and sequences the recorded images for the viewer. The idea of the camera narrator is not revolutionary, but in the context of what I propose with speculative vision, it plays a key role in understanding the way vision works both within the cinematic world of a text and within the real world of the audience.

My conceptualization of speculative vision hinges on some of the central ways POV is established within cinematic texts: the objective, subjective, and extraordinary positions. If we consider the objective first, we can equate that with the camera as narrator but also a character and/or audience position. For Orna Ravis in Ethics of Cinematic Experience: Screens of Alterity (2019), “the possibility that another is watching is already contained in the impersonal [objective] point of view” (31). This gives rise to the voyeuristic qualities of cinema; we are gazing onto a world, whether strange like many speculative media texts or familiar like many realist texts, through the “eyes” of another, a window that merely allows provides a frame for our consumption, restricting us to what it wants us to know/see.

Subjective positions are what we traditionally consider when interpreting point of view. These shots mimic the field of vision of a character and attempt to provide some deeper insight into their internal states of being. This concept does not limit itself to visual media like film, but all representational media that is character based. According to Smith, “Where a fiction focuses on the quality of a character’s experience, imagining what the character thinks and feels is often integral to the process of working out what is true in the fiction, since not everything is or indeed can be literally spelt out—for aesthetic as well as logical reason” (420). At this point is where I find fault with Smith and others who hold tightly to the idea of identification being a key component of subjective point-of-view. Although still a young medium in comparison to literature and drama, contemporary audiences, particularly those of speculative media products, have been conditioned to understand that the representation of characters, settings, and other aspects are filtered and true identification, particularly with characters, cannot be achieved because of the ways that visual media distorts the human vision—after all it is a technology of seeing rather than a natural method of seeing. Cinema’s exteriority establishes a distance between viewer and character. Raviv concludes similarly: “Cinema can get no closer than an aesthetic representation of inner states and does not actually show us how a character sees in some frame of mind. It does not show us, the film viewers, what the character is seeing as he actually sees it but only a construction of the film narrator or a cinematic convention” (35). Although cinema can only seek to present the exteriority of a character’s internal states through artistic framing, the camera does mimic the literary technique of free indirect discourse by providing us with shot-reverse-shot sequences when inhabiting a character’s field of vision, yet this use of free indirect discourse further complicates the ways subjective positions affect viewers because it relies upon the “vision” of narrator and authorial/auteurial identity composing the text.

My use of the term “extraordinary point of view” is borrowed heavily from Raviv. She asserts that the extraordinary point-of-view plays into “cinema’s greatest advantage: its ability to transcend ordinary human perception,” and that this POV position stems from the technological capabilities of the camera to capture and manipulate the field of vision beyond natural human qualities (37). Raviv points toward the use of close-ups and time distortions like slow or fast motion as key examples of the extraordinary point-of-view, and through this ability to break with the natural elements of human sight, the extraordinary point-of-view established via the camera delineates “a new way of seeing” (38). Ultimately, Raviv concludes, “The basic structure of the cinematic gaze is […] not simply a point of view but […] a plurality” (43, emphasis in original). It is here that I connect my conception of speculative vision. Because cinematic “seeing” is composed of not only technological elements but multiple perspectives, it is strange within itself; it provides us as viewers with the ability to glimpse images outside of our bodies yet connect them back to make us feel and understand larger meanings from the text. The merging of objective, subjective, and extraordinary POVs and authorial elements within the cinematic text creates a unique engagement with the field of vision, and through that unique engagement, we can enter a complex system of meaning making that allows us to witness things out-of-the-ordinary to disrupt complacency in the ordinary.

(Slide 2) So where does Jordan Peele and his films fit into this? Well, he is heavily immersed in the speculative genre, merging elements of horror and science fiction with realism. There is no denying the cultural power of Peele’s writer-director debut Get Out (2017); its massive success opened new areas for Black horror with white audiences and garnered tremendous critical acclaim, earning Oscar nominations for Best Picture, Best Original Screenplay, which Peele won, Best Director, and Best Actor in 2018. In a profile in The New York Times Magazine in 2017, Morris Wesley claims Peele “gave us language we didn’t know we lacked” around the topic of racism and that “he’s using genre as an instrument of self-discovery and social X-ray.” In the Introduction to a special issue of the academic journal Black Camera, David Wall extends this claim by stating that Peele “does what great directors in the American tradition have always done, which is to take generic conventions and tropes and elevate them into films that engage with profound issues of politics, culture, and history” (182). All three of Peele’s films; Get Out, Us (2019), and Nope (2022); which Wall calls “the ‘Looking Trilogy’” (182), center the main narratives around some element of vision. Even in the theatrical posters for each film, we can see the strong focus on eyes and sight. The poster for Get Out presents us with multiple eyelines. From Rose’s gazes toward Chris in the upper left to the low angle shot of Chris looking down at something out of frame in the bottom left to the almost pupil and eye formed by the fractured glass, the poster forces the viewer into the perspective of witnessing; the viewer is going to bear witness to gain understanding. Us’s poster does not present the gaze as prominently; however, if we look closely at the scissors in center frame, we can distinguish two visual symbols of looking: the representation of two bodies facing in different directions, which is formed by each piece, and two skull-like eye sockets, which are formed by the bow. Here with get the idea of looking away and the death stare, which highlights how our eyes are used to ignore but also confront. Finally, Nope’s poster places the viewer in a familiar perspective—the objective position—by having us “look up” at the cloud trailing a string of flags just above the horizon line. We as viewers are forced to turn our eyes upward, indicating that the power of directional looking will be central to this narrative world.

Because Get Out and Us have been critiqued more fully in academic discourse, particularly around themes of race, I will refer to them briefly as precursors to ideas expressed in Nope. I want to place Nope under further analysis to reveal how Peele and cinematographer Hoyte van Hoytema use a plurality of points-of-view to place the audience within a field of speculative vision. This field of speculative vision in Nope provides Black characters and the audience a weapon against the destructive power of Capitalist whiteness in not only the entertainment but society writ large.

(Slide 3) The act of being “seen” or captured by the eye, whether it be natural or mechanical, features heavily in Peele’s films. The infamous party scene in Get Out provides us with direct reference to the power of mechanical vision in Peele’s cinema. In these three screenshots, Chris, played by Daniel Kaluuya, captures the Armitages and their guest with his camera. Each shot is subjective and extraordinary because they seek to place the viewer in Chris’s perspective, but the shots work in a doubly mechanical vision, filtered through the literal film camera and the figurative photography camera. Chris uses this mechanical field of vision to highlight the oddness of the people and environment, drawing our attention to the racism contained within these supposedly “liberal” spaces. The grid lines act like a scope in a gun because the camera becomes a powerful weapon for Chris to fight against the affluent whites who feign admiration through their not-so-veiled microaggressions. The zoom in on Logan, aka Andre (Lakeith Stanfield), the only other Black man at the party, alludes to a semblance of unity and community within hostile environments for historically marginalized peoples; however, because Peele creates this shot through the mechanical gaze, this speculative field of vision further alienates Chris, forcing him to question not only his role but also the role of Black people within the larger narrative structures of the film.

(Slide 4) Nope uses the mechanical gaze in a different manner than Get Out, but it still maintains a strong speculative field of vision. The two screenshots here illustrate the ways the mechanical gaze functions at various points in the narrative. Nope’s heavy focus on spectacle and the whitewashing of the entertainment industry allows Peele and van Hoytema to use the camera in the first two acts not as a weapon for potential salvation but as a weapon of the Capitalist machine, like it has historically been. In “Jordan Peele’s Nope: Saying No to the Society of the Spectacle,” Keith Booker and Isra Daraiseh claim that “the viewers of films (including this one) [referring to Nope] and, by extension, the audiences of all contemporary media, are so ubiquitous that almost everyone in modern America, via mechanisms such as social media, has a sense of being on display for an audience” (172). Additionally, Hilary MezaGuerra, in “Afrofuturist Horror on the Western Frontier: Capitalism’s Reckoning and Queer Possibilities in Jordan Peele’s Nope,” describes the film as “a cautionary tale about the violent dangers of indulgent exploitation for Nope’s characters and viewers alike” (125). Jupe, played by Steven Yuen, is a prime example of the negative effects of the mechanical gaze. The left image shows Jupe during the filming of Gordy’s Home. The camera eye captures Jupe gazing up as balloons rise from a box. Although this may seem innocuous, the scene not only foreshadows Jupe looking up at Jean Jacket, what MezaGuerra calls “a metaphor for whiteness […] and […] its attendant structural institutions” (125-6), as it consumes him and the other audience members at his Wild West Show in the main timeline of the film, but also this shot reveals the subtly the way Jupe is enraptured by the spectacle. Placing him just outside of the center grid line and to the right of the frame, Peele and van Hoytema position Jupe as a curious and aspirational character, one who cannot see his own exploitation at the hands of a white Capitalist industry; Jupe’s looking up, ultimately, leads to his death and expulsion from the white Capitalist world.

However, Nope does not limit its destructive vision to solely people of color, although as I’ll point out later, they are the primary targets. The right image is another example of a subjective and extraordinary POV shot, this time from Holst’s (Michael Wincott) adoption of the mechanical gaze. Unlike Chris in Get Out, Holst’s identity as a white man, more specifically the representation of the white auteur in the cinema industry, does not grant him access to the disruptive power of the camera. As the auteur, Holst is firmly embedded in the exploitative nature of white Capitalism; we see him at points in the film capturing O.J. Jr., played by Daniel Kaluuya, for a commercial and at his home editing film footage of exotic animals. As he leaves to capture Jean Jacket on film alone, Holst says, “We don’t deserve the impossible” (Peele, Nope). In combination with the image displayed here, we can possibly read this as Holst sacrificing himself for art; however, I contend that Peele intentional places Holst within a speculative field of vision. The low angle shot from the mechanical gaze of the camera combined with Holst’s screams as Jean Jacket devours him implies punishment for deviance. Because Holst uses his auteurism to aid the non-white characters in their efforts to record horror and atrocity, he becomes expendable to white Capitalism’s mission of gaining sustenance from exploitation.

(Slide 5) Peele and van Hoyetma extend their use of speculative vision beyond the replication of mechanical gazes within the structure of the film’s narrative and plot. They use the camera’s narratorial ability to reveal the strangeness of the “real” world. As we’ve seen, the medium of cinema is firmly embedded in white Capitalism and has historically abused people of color. This is evident in Nope when Peele chooses to frame the story of the Haywoods—O.J. Jr. and Emerald, played by Keke Palmer—with Muybridge’s The Horse in Motion. The context allows Peele and van Hoytema to situate shots in the larger history of the exploitation and destruction of Black bodies by the cinema industry. The two images we see on this slide are part of that context. The image on the right is from early in the film after Pops (Keith David) is killed from falling debris, initially thought to be from a plane but later revealed to be the wasted from Jean Jacket. Pops is struck in the eye by a nickel, and Peele does not pull the symbolic nature of this. As we can see, Pops eye is destroyed by literal money falling from the sky, meaning that blessings come with a steep cost, particularly for people of color. The overhead close-up shot presents the viewer with an extraordinary perspective, one not available to us in natural vision, making it fall inside the field of speculative vision. (Don’t read: One cannot help but think about the colloquialism “If I had a nickel every time…, I’d have two nickels,” highlighting the rarity and strangeness of certain things.)

The close-up of O.J. Jr. on the right highlights another destructive and exploitative element of the entertainment industry—that of the minstrel figure. The use of blackface by white actors represented the stereotypes and dehumanization of Black people in the 19th century. This not only continued a long history of misrepresentation, but it also promoted a continual devaluing of Black artistic talent in the burgeoning entertainment industry of the U.S. Peele and van Hoytema choose to exploit Kaluuya’s natural features through lighting in this extraordinary perspective by the narratorial camera. Their accentuation of Kaluuya’s white eyes, pursed lips, deep skin color mimics the coal black makeup and exaggerated features of minstrel performers. This extraordinary perspective occurs as O.J. hides from the consuming and expelling “eye” of Jean Jacket. The adoption of historical exploitation provides O.J. with a way to avoid being detected by the vision of white Capitalism; by disparaging O.J. with this close-up, Peele and van Hoytema grant him the ability to survive and fight against the system that seeks to consume and profit from misrepresentation.

(Slide 6) Along with the use of the extraordinary perspective of close-ups to present the field of speculative vision, Peele and van Hoytema position the narratorial camera in subservient positions when Jean Jacket is present in the world. The three screenshots in this slide show the placement of the camera in low angle positions. When the “eye” of Jean Jacket enters the frame, Peele and van Hoytema have the camera break from the character and move on its own. This movement creates a strange perspective. No longer are we fully attached to the subjective perspective of a character like O.J. Jr. nor are we attached to a fully objective perspective; having the camera float away and seek out its own line of sight to gaze up in Jean Jacket’s “eye” implicates the viewer in feeding the spectacle and risking being consumed by its sight.

(Slide 7) In an interview with Cream, Peele describes that Nope “is about capturing the impossible on camera.” With speculative vision, him and van Hoytema accomplish making the strange familiar.
